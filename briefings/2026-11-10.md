GROWTH EQUITY 101: Anduril's tender offer is a good way to learn how these deals work. A company picks a price per share, usually anchored to its last funding round, and invites employees and early investors to sell a capped dollar amount of stock to new or existing backers; the company itself never touches the money. Firms do this because employees who have been vesting stock for years want cash before an IPO that may be years away, and a tender keeps them from quitting just to get it. Asking an investor whether they've bought into a tender, and at what discount or premium to the last round, is a sharp way to learn how eager they are to get into a hot company.

LEARN ONE THING

Anthropic's 22-bank underwriting group is a good way to learn how an IPO syndicate [banks hired to sell a stock to investors] is organized. The "lead-left" bank, here Goldman Sachs, gets top billing on the prospectus and runs the deal day to day, deciding pricing and allocating shares; other "bookrunners" like Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan share that work and the biggest fees, while the rest are smaller "co-managers" added mainly for research coverage and distribution reach. The bigger and more contested the deal, the more banks want a piece, which is why a trillion-dollar IPO like Anthropic's attracts 22 names instead of two or three.
